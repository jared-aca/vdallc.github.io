Secondary Service Connection: Connecting a New Condition to a VA Disability

A disability can qualify when a service-connected condition causes or aggravates it. Learn the evidence VA evaluates under 38 C.F.R. § 3.310.

A disability can qualify when a service-connected condition causes or aggravates it. Learn the evidence VA evaluates under 38 C.F.R. § 3.310. The outcome of a VA disability claim depends on the specific evidence, the governing regulation, and the procedural history. This guide explains the practical questions to examine before deciding what to file.

The two paths: causation and aggravation

Secondary service connection recognizes that a disability can result from another condition VA has already service connected. Under 38 C.F.R. § 3.310, the question may be whether the primary disability caused the new condition or whether it aggravated the new condition beyond its natural progression. Those are distinct medical theories and should not be treated as interchangeable.

For example, a service-connected orthopedic injury might alter gait and contribute to another joint problem. A clinician should explain the mechanism and the particular veteran's medical history rather than simply assert that one diagnosis commonly accompanies another.

The evidence you need

The record ordinarily must establish a current secondary disability, an already service-connected primary condition, and a competent link between them. The link may come from medical literature, clinical records, a reasoned medical opinion, or a combination of evidence. Medical articles alone seldom establish the relationship in an individual case without explanation of how the research applies.

Lay statements can establish observable symptoms, onset, and changes in function. They usually cannot replace a specialized medical explanation when causation involves complex physiology.

Aggravation is not the same as causation

A condition can predate the primary disability and still be aggravated by it. Under the aggravation provisions of § 3.310, VA considers baseline severity and the degree of additional disability attributable to the service-connected condition. Records from before the worsening and during the worsening can be especially useful.

A medical opinion that only answers 'Did PTSD cause sleep apnea?' may leave unanswered whether PTSD-related factors aggravated sleep apnea. The adequacy of an opinion depends on the actual theories raised and facts of the case.

Practical takeaway: Before choosing a review option or submitting more evidence, compare the rating decision with the records VA actually considered. The most useful next step addresses the precise missing legal or factual element.

Common secondary-claim examples

Examples that may warrant individualized medical analysis include radiculopathy associated with a lumbar spine disability, headaches associated with a service-connected neck condition, gastrointestinal effects of medication prescribed for a service-connected disability, and psychiatric consequences of chronic pain. None of these connections is automatic. Diagnoses, mechanisms, alternative causes, and medical chronology matter.

Be wary of online lists promising that a specific diagnosis always produces a secondary award. VA must still determine whether the facts satisfy the legal and medical requirements.

Avoid duplicate compensation

A veteran may have several diagnoses but cannot receive separate ratings for the same manifestations when doing so would constitute pyramiding under 38 C.F.R. § 4.14. On the other hand, distinct neurological, orthopedic, or other manifestations may warrant separate evaluations when the rating schedule permits it. The issue is overlap of symptoms, not simply overlap of diagnoses.

Review both the service-connection theory and the eventual evaluation. Winning secondary service connection does not guarantee a particular percentage.

Building a persuasive submission

Describe the primary service-connected condition, when the secondary symptoms began, how the symptoms changed, what treating clinicians observed, and what other plausible causes exist. Ask a qualified clinician to explain the relationship using case-specific facts and clear reasoning. If VA denied the claim for lack of nexus, obtain evidence addressing that precise deficiency.

Keep track of the date the secondary claim was filed. The effective date for a newly claimed secondary condition is not automatically the same as the effective date of the primary disability.

Questions to ask before you file

  • What exactly did VA concede in its favorable findings, and what element remains disputed?
  • Which evidence was already in the record when VA made the decision?
  • Is the issue a legal or factual mistake, missing development, or a genuine lack of supporting evidence?
  • What is the date of the decision notice, and which review deadline applies?
  • Would the proposed evidence answer the specific reason VA gave for its decision?
