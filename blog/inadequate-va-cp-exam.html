What Makes a VA C&P Exam Inadequate?

A negative C&P opinion is not automatically an adequate one. Learn what to look for when an examiner uses incorrect facts, ignores important evidence, or fails to answer the medical question.

What Makes a VA C&P Examination Inadequate?

A negative C&P examination can become one of the most important pieces of evidence in a VA disability claim. But VA is not entitled to rely on an examination simply because an examiner checked a box and wrote a conclusion.

When VA obtains a medical examination or opinion, the report must contain enough information to allow the claim to be decided properly. Under 38 C.F.R. § 4.2, if an examination report does not contain sufficient detail, the rating board is responsible for returning it as inadequate for evaluation purposes.

An unfavorable opinion and an inadequate opinion are not the same thing. An examiner can disagree with the veteran and still provide an adequate opinion. The problem arises when the opinion is based on incorrect facts, fails to answer the medical question, ignores important evidence, or does not provide enough reasoning to support its conclusion.

1. The Examiner Used the Wrong Facts

Medical reasoning is only as reliable as the factual foundation beneath it. An opinion may be defective when the examiner misunderstands the veteran's service history, dates the onset of symptoms incorrectly, overlooks a documented injury, or states that evidence does not exist when it is actually in the record.

This is why veterans should read the factual history in the C&P report carefully. If the examiner's conclusion depends on a fact that is demonstrably wrong, identify the specific record that contradicts it.

2. The Examiner Did Not Provide a Meaningful Rationale

A medical opinion should do more than announce a result. The examiner should explain the medical reasoning connecting the facts to the conclusion.

Statements such as "there is no nexus," "the medical literature does not support a relationship," or "there is no evidence of chronicity" may be incomplete if the examiner does not explain why those observations resolve the veteran's particular medical question.

The Court has emphasized that rationale is an essential part of evaluating competing medical opinions. The report should be understandable when read as a whole and should provide enough detail for VA to make an informed decision.

3. The Examiner Ignored Relevant Evidence

An examiner does not need to discuss every page in a claims file. But the opinion should account for evidence that materially affects the medical question.

That can include favorable treatment records, diagnostic testing, an earlier diagnosis, a private nexus opinion, relevant service records, or another piece of evidence that conflicts with the examiner's factual assumptions.

If a favorable private opinion is already in the record, a later examiner should not simply act as though it does not exist when the competing medical reasoning matters to the issue being decided.

4. The Examiner Failed to Address Lay Statements

Veterans are often competent to report observable symptoms and their history. A veteran can describe when ringing in the ears began, when back pain started, how frequently migraines occur, or what happens during a flare-up.

An examiner may evaluate those statements in light of the medical evidence, but simply ignoring them can leave the opinion incomplete. This is especially important when the negative opinion is based primarily on the absence of treatment records.

5. The Examiner Answered the Wrong Question

Sometimes an examination contains pages of medical information but never answers the question VA actually needs resolved.

For direct service connection, the issue may be whether the current disability is related to an in-service injury, disease, event, or exposure. For secondary service connection, the question may concern causation, aggravation, or both.

An opinion addressing a different theory does not necessarily resolve the theory actually raised by the claim.

6. The Examiner Addressed Causation but Ignored Aggravation

This is a recurring problem in secondary service-connection claims.

An examiner might write that sleep apnea was not caused by a service-connected condition and then end the analysis. If the record also raises whether the service-connected disability aggravated the sleep apnea, the causation opinion alone may not answer the entire medical question.

When reviewing a secondary opinion, look for separate consideration of causation and aggravation when both theories apply.

7. The Examiner Relied Only on the Absence of Treatment

A gap in treatment can be relevant, but it does not automatically establish that symptoms did not exist. Veterans may self-treat, lack access to care, avoid reporting symptoms, or simply not seek treatment for every problem during service.

If the examiner's entire rationale is that the service treatment records are silent, compare that rationale with the veteran's statements and the rest of the record. The medical opinion should address the actual history presented by the evidence.

8. The Examination Does Not Contain Enough Detail to Rate the Disability

Sometimes the problem is not service connection at all. The examination may be inadequate because it fails to provide the findings VA needs to determine the proper rating.

38 C.F.R. § 4.2 specifically requires sufficient detail for evaluation purposes. Depending on the disability, missing information about functional impairment, range of motion, flare-ups, frequency, severity, occupational effects, or required testing can matter.

The exact information required depends on the condition and the applicable rating criteria.

9. The Findings Are Internally Inconsistent

Read the entire DBQ, not just the medical opinion section. Sometimes one part of the examination contradicts another.

An examiner may document significant functional impairment but later state that the condition has no occupational impact. A report may document recurring symptoms while the rationale assumes that no symptoms existed. Those inconsistencies may require explanation before the report can reliably support a decision.

10. The Examiner Did Not Address Favorable Medical Literature

Submitting an article does not automatically prove a nexus. But when relevant medical literature is part of the evidentiary argument, an examiner's generic statement that "medical literature does not support" the claimed relationship may deserve closer scrutiny.

The question is whether the examiner engaged with the medical issue presented by the veteran's evidence and explained why the literature does or does not apply to the veteran's individual circumstances.

What Should You Do With a Bad C&P Examination?

Start by obtaining and reading the examination report and medical opinion. Compare the examiner's factual statements with the evidence in the claims file. Identify specific errors rather than simply arguing that the examination was unfair.

Depending on the procedural posture of the claim, the appropriate response may involve submitting additional evidence, challenging the adequacy of the examination, seeking correction of a duty-to-assist error, or pursuing another available review option.

The important point is specificity. "The examiner was wrong" is an argument. "The examiner stated there were no complaints of knee pain in service, but the service treatment record dated May 14, 2011 documents knee pain after a training injury" identifies a concrete factual problem that can be evaluated.

The Bottom Line

A C&P examination does not become adequate merely because VA ordered it. The report should be based on an accurate history, address the relevant evidence and medical questions, contain sufficient detail, and provide medical reasoning that allows VA to make an informed decision.

If a denial depends heavily on a negative C&P opinion, reviewing the examination itself is often one of the first steps in determining what went wrong and what evidence or argument may be needed next.
