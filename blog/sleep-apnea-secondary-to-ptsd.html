Sleep Apnea Secondary to PTSD: How to Establish Service Connection

Learn what VA requires to connect obstructive sleep apnea to service-connected PTSD, including medical nexus opinions and aggravation evidence.

A veteran with service-connected PTSD and obstructive sleep apnea may wonder whether the second condition can be recognized as secondary to the first. The answer is that secondary service connection is possible when supported by the facts and competent evidence, but VA does not presume that PTSD causes sleep apnea.

The Legal Standard for Secondary Service Connection

Under 38 C.F.R. § 3.310, a disability may be service connected when it is proximately due to or the result of an established service-connected condition. Secondary service connection also encompasses qualifying aggravation, meaning worsening attributable to the service-connected condition rather than merely the natural progression of the non-service-connected disease.

A claim should clearly identify whether the theory is causation, aggravation, or both. An examiner who addresses only whether PTSD caused sleep apnea may leave the separate aggravation question unanswered.

Why PTSD and Sleep Apnea Claims Are Difficult

PTSD can involve disturbed sleep, insomnia, nightmares, and other symptoms. Obstructive sleep apnea, however, involves recurrent upper-airway obstruction during sleep. Sleep disturbance alone is not proof of obstructive sleep apnea or proof that PTSD caused it.

Research exploring associations between PTSD and sleep apnea may provide context, but a population-level association is not automatically a medical nexus for an individual veteran.

Evidence That Can Strengthen the Claim

Start with the PTSD award, a valid sleep apnea diagnosis, and sleep-study results. A clinician’s opinion should review the veteran’s timeline, risk factors, relevant treatment, and any proposed mechanisms. If medications, weight changes, or another intermediate step are relevant, the opinion must explain the causal chain rather than assume it.

A persuasive medical opinion should address alternative causes, explain the evidence for the specific veteran, and distinguish a possible connection from one that is at least as likely as not.

Aggravation Must Be Considered Separately

Even where a medical professional concludes that PTSD did not cause sleep apnea, it may be necessary to consider whether the service-connected condition aggravated it. The opinion should discuss what changed, the medical basis for attributing worsening to PTSD or its treatment, and what the records show.

A bare statement that sleep apnea is anatomical and therefore cannot be related to PTSD may be inadequate if it fails to answer a reasonably raised aggravation theory.

How to Respond to a Denial

Read the medical opinion carefully. Did it discuss the veteran’s records, medication history, and both causation and aggravation? Did VA address favorable private evidence? Did the examiner rely only on a generalized proposition without discussing the veteran’s facts?

The strongest appeal identifies a specific evidentiary or legal deficiency and supplies the missing proof where needed, rather than simply repeating that PTSD and sleep apnea frequently occur together.

What to Do Before Filing or Appealing

A carefully framed secondary claim should ask the right medical questions and avoid promising that a PTSD diagnosis automatically qualifies a veteran for sleep apnea benefits.

Keep the rating decision and notification letter, identify the exact issue under dispute, collect the relevant records, and review the deadline before selecting a review option. Evidence that answers VA’s specific reason for denial is more useful than a stack of unrelated documents.
