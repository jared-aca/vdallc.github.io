Clear and Unmistakable Error (CUE) in VA Decisions: A Practical Guide

CUE is not a second chance to reweigh evidence. Understand the demanding legal standard, finality, and how to identify a specific outcome-changing error.

CUE is not a second chance to reweigh evidence. Understand the demanding legal standard, finality, and how to identify a specific outcome-changing error. The outcome of a VA disability claim depends on the specific evidence, the governing regulation, and the procedural history. This guide explains the practical questions to examine before deciding what to file.

Why CUE is different from an ordinary appeal

Clear and unmistakable error is a narrow way to seek revision of a final VA decision. It is not a general complaint that the earlier rater made a poor judgment call. The claimant must identify an error of fact or law based on the record and law applicable at the time, and explain why the result would have been manifestly different without that error.

For final agency decisions, 38 C.F.R. § 3.105(a) governs revision based on CUE. Board decisions have separate CUE procedures under 38 U.S.C. § 7111 and 38 C.F.R. Part 20. Confusing those pathways can derail an otherwise serious allegation.

Three elements of a meaningful CUE theory

First, identify the precise factual or legal mistake. Second, establish that the error is undebatable under the law and record then existing, not simply one reasonable interpretation among several. Third, show that correcting the error would necessarily have changed the outcome. An error that would only have triggered additional development generally does not satisfy this demanding standard.

A statement that 'VA should have rated me higher' is not enough. A stronger allegation identifies the specific diagnostic code, evidence then before VA, the applicable regulation, and the outcome compelled by those facts.

Why a failure in the duty to assist usually is not CUE

An incomplete record can support a timely appeal or a duty-to-assist challenge, but failure to obtain an examination or records generally is not CUE. The reason is that missing development does not establish what the missing evidence would have shown. CUE review does not ordinarily permit constructing a better record with later medical evidence.

Similarly, disagreement over how VA weighed competing medical opinions is usually not CUE. If reasonable adjudicators could reach different conclusions from the record, the error is not undebatable.

Practical takeaway: Before choosing a review option or submitting more evidence, compare the rating decision with the records VA actually considered. The most useful next step addresses the precise missing legal or factual element.

Historical law matters

A 2010 rating decision must be analyzed using the law and evidence applicable to that 2010 decision. Applying a modern rating criterion retroactively without legal authority can produce an invalid theory. Changes in presumptions, diagnostic codes, and interpretations may affect current claims without proving that a decades-old final decision was unmistakably erroneous.

Before drafting, retrieve the complete rating decision, evidence list, service records, and the historical version of the relevant regulation. The exact decision date can determine whether a particular legal provision applied.

Examples of potentially stronger and weaker arguments

Potentially stronger: a final decision expressly acknowledges an undisputed fact and then applies a regulation that plainly excludes that fact, with only one legally possible result. Potentially weaker: an examiner failed to discuss symptoms, the rater preferred a VA opinion over a private opinion, or the veteran now has a more persuasive nexus letter. The latter issues may justify another procedural route, but not necessarily CUE.

A favorable finding made in a later decision is not automatically proof that an older final decision contained CUE. Each decision has its own record and applicable law.

How to present a focused CUE request

Identify the exact final decision and issue, quote or accurately summarize the rule that was in force, identify the evidence already in the file, and explain the manifestly different outcome. Avoid stacking unrelated theories into one vague paragraph. If an earlier decision is still within an appeal window, an ordinary decision-review option may be more appropriate than a collateral CUE attack.

Effective dates and revision consequences can be significant, which is why CUE arguments deserve especially careful review by a qualified accredited representative.

Questions to ask before you file

  • What exactly did VA concede in its favorable findings, and what element remains disputed?
  • Which evidence was already in the record when VA made the decision?
  • Is the issue a legal or factual mistake, missing development, or a genuine lack of supporting evidence?
  • What is the date of the decision notice, and which review deadline applies?
  • Would the proposed evidence answer the specific reason VA gave for its decision?
