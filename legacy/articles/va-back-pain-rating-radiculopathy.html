VA Back Pain Ratings: Motion, Flare-Ups, and Radiculopathy

Understand how VA evaluates thoracolumbar spine limitations, painful motion, flare-ups, and separate neurological manifestations.

Understand how VA evaluates thoracolumbar spine limitations, painful motion, flare-ups, and separate neurological manifestations. The outcome of a VA disability claim depends on the specific evidence, the governing regulation, and the procedural history. This guide explains the practical questions to examine before deciding what to file.

What VA measures in a spine examination

Most common lumbar spine conditions are evaluated under the General Rating Formula for Diseases and Injuries of the Spine in 38 C.F.R. § 4.71a. Range of motion is central: the examiner measures forward flexion, combined motion, and other findings, and VA considers whether there is qualifying muscle spasm, guarding, abnormal gait, or abnormal spinal contour.

A diagnosis such as degenerative disc disease does not by itself determine the percentage. Two veterans with similar imaging may have very different functional loss.

Thoracolumbar rating landmarks

Under the general formula, thoracolumbar forward flexion greater than 60 degrees but not greater than 85 degrees can support 10%; greater than 30 but not greater than 60 degrees can support 20%; and flexion to 30 degrees or less can support 40%, subject to the full criteria. Higher levels generally require specified unfavorable ankylosis. These are legal thresholds, not a substitute for reviewing the complete schedule.

The examiner should not treat a veteran's first measured movement as the entire picture when pain, repeated use, or flare-ups create additional functional limitation.

Flare-ups and repeated use over time

A person may bend farther on a relatively good examination day than during recurring flare-ups. VA examinations should address functional loss from pain, weakness, fatigability, incoordination, and repeated use as applicable. A credible description of how often flares occur, how long they last, what triggers them, and what movements become impossible can help the examiner estimate additional loss.

Write down concrete limitations: inability to tie shoes, difficulty sitting longer than a certain period, missed work, or needing assistance during a flare. The question is not simply whether pain exists, but what it prevents you from doing.

Practical takeaway: Before choosing a review option or submitting more evidence, compare the rating decision with the records VA actually considered. The most useful next step addresses the precise missing legal or factual element.

Radiculopathy can be separately evaluated

Radiating pain, numbness, tingling, weakness, or reflex changes may indicate nerve involvement. The spine formula instructs VA to evaluate associated objective neurological abnormalities separately under an appropriate diagnostic code. The correct nerve, side, and severity matter; not every complaint of leg pain automatically establishes radiculopathy.

An examiner may document straight-leg raising, sensory loss, strength deficits, or reflex findings. The veteran should also describe whether symptoms extend below the knee, affect the foot, or interfere with walking or balance.

Intervertebral disc syndrome and incapacitating episodes

Certain cases involving intervertebral disc syndrome may be evaluated under the formula based on incapacitating episodes if that produces the higher evaluation. Under the regulation, an incapacitating episode has a specific definition involving acute signs and symptoms requiring bed rest prescribed by a physician and treatment by a physician. Self-imposed rest alone does not meet that definition.

Review the medical records before assuming a week spent in bed counts as a qualifying episode. The documentation and applicable diagnostic criteria control.

How to challenge an incomplete back examination

Look for range-of-motion values, discussion of painful movement, estimates for flare-ups and repeated use, neurological findings, and the examiner's explanation. If an examiner states that estimating flare-related loss is impossible without explaining why, the adequacy of the opinion may require closer scrutiny.

A veteran who has developed new neurological symptoms or worsening motion limitation may need an increased-rating claim; a veteran disputing a recent rating may need timely decision review. The procedural choice affects evidence and effective dates.

Questions to ask before you file

  • What exactly did VA concede in its favorable findings, and what element remains disputed?
  • Which evidence was already in the record when VA made the decision?
  • Is the issue a legal or factual mistake, missing development, or a genuine lack of supporting evidence?
  • What is the date of the decision notice, and which review deadline applies?
  • Would the proposed evidence answer the specific reason VA gave for its decision?
