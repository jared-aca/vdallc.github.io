VA Duty to Assist Errors: When VA Fails to Develop Your Claim

VA must make reasonable efforts to obtain certain evidence and provide examinations when required. Learn how to identify a genuine pre-decisional development error.

VA must make reasonable efforts to obtain certain evidence and provide examinations when required. Learn how to identify a genuine pre-decisional development error. The outcome of a VA disability claim depends on the specific evidence, the governing regulation, and the procedural history. This guide explains the practical questions to examine before deciding what to file.

What the duty to assist actually requires

VA's duty to assist is a legal obligation, not a promise that every claim will be granted. Under 38 U.S.C. § 5103A and 38 C.F.R. § 3.159, VA generally must make reasonable efforts to obtain relevant records and provide a medical examination or opinion when the statutory criteria are met. The precise duties depend on the type of claim, the information provided, and the record already available.

The important distinction is between evidence VA had a duty to seek and evidence that would merely have been helpful. VA is not required to order an examination in every claim, and a veteran's disagreement with a medical conclusion is not by itself proof of a duty-to-assist error.

Missing service and federal records

Service treatment records, relevant personnel records, and identified federal medical records can establish an in-service event or show symptoms long before a civilian diagnosis. If VA made a decision without adequately pursuing relevant federal records, the development may be deficient. A veteran should identify the unit, installation, approximate dates, treatment facility, or deployment involved.

If records cannot be located, VA has notice obligations. An absence of documentation in an incomplete file should not be treated as conclusive proof that an event never occurred. At the same time, missing records do not automatically establish the claimed event or medical nexus.

Private records and the veteran’s role

VA can help request private treatment records when the veteran identifies the provider and provides required authorization, often on VA Forms 21-4142 and 21-4142a. Incomplete provider information or missing authorization can prevent a request. Keep copies of records yourself where possible, and identify why each provider's records matter.

A private physician's record may document years of symptoms, a diagnosis, or treatment soon after discharge. The value lies in its relevance to the denied element, not simply the number of pages submitted.

Practical takeaway: Before choosing a review option or submitting more evidence, compare the rating decision with the records VA actually considered. The most useful next step addresses the precise missing legal or factual element.

When an examination or medical opinion is required

For an original service-connection claim, the examination threshold considers evidence of current disability or persistent symptoms, evidence establishing a relevant service event, an indication the condition may be associated with service, and whether the file otherwise contains sufficient medical evidence. This is not the same as already proving the claim. Credible lay evidence can matter, depending on the facts.

If an exam is provided, adequacy matters. An opinion that ignores material medical history, relies on an inaccurate factual premise, or supplies no understandable reasoning may require correction. But a negative opinion is not automatically inadequate merely because it is unfavorable.

What happens after an HLR finds a pre-decisional error

A Higher-Level Review can identify a duty-to-assist error that existed before the decision being reviewed. VA typically closes the HLR issue and establishes a separate process to obtain the missing evidence, such as records or a new examination, before issuing a new decision. This is a development correction, not an award of benefits.

The veteran should attend requested examinations, verify contact information, and monitor for notices. A correction can still result in a denial if the completed evidence does not support entitlement.

How to articulate the error precisely

A useful argument names the missing action and why the record triggered it: 'The service records documented repeated ankle complaints, the claim identified ongoing ankle pain, and VA denied without obtaining a medical examination or opinion.' Compare that with a vague statement that 'VA failed its duty to assist.' Specificity gives the reviewer a concrete record-based issue to resolve.

Review the decision date carefully. Evidence created after that decision ordinarily cannot establish that VA committed a pre-decisional duty-to-assist error by failing to consider it. Later evidence may instead support a Supplemental Claim.

Questions to ask before you file

  • What exactly did VA concede in its favorable findings, and what element remains disputed?
  • Which evidence was already in the record when VA made the decision?
  • Is the issue a legal or factual mistake, missing development, or a genuine lack of supporting evidence?
  • What is the date of the decision notice, and which review deadline applies?
  • Would the proposed evidence answer the specific reason VA gave for its decision?
