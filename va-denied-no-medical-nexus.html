VA Denied Your Claim for Lack of Medical Nexus: What Next?

A current diagnosis and an in-service event do not always establish service connection. Learn how to identify the missing medical link and respond strategically.

A current diagnosis and an in-service event do not always establish service connection. Learn how to identify the missing medical link and respond strategically. The outcome of a VA disability claim depends on the specific evidence, the governing regulation, and the procedural history. This guide explains the practical questions to examine before deciding what to file.

What a medical nexus actually establishes

For many direct service-connection claims, VA must find a current disability, a qualifying in-service disease, injury, or event, and a relationship between the two. That relationship is often called the medical nexus. A veteran may have extensive treatment records proving the current diagnosis and still be denied if the evidence does not connect it to service.

Read the favorable findings before responding. If VA already conceded the current disability and an in-service event, the dispute may be much narrower than the denial letter initially suggests.

Why a bare conclusion is weak evidence

An opinion stating only that a condition 'is at least as likely as not related to service' may be less persuasive if it does not explain why. A useful opinion identifies the relevant medical facts, onset and course of symptoms, exposure or injury, applicable medical principles, and why alternative explanations are more or less likely.

A clinician need not use magic words to be persuasive. What matters is whether the reasoning permits the adjudicator to understand the conclusion and evaluate it against contrary evidence.

Review the VA examiner’s factual assumptions

Some negative opinions rest on an assertion that no relevant event occurred, no symptoms were reported, or no treatment happened for many years. Compare those assertions against the actual service records, personnel history, lay statements, and post-service records. An opinion built on a materially inaccurate factual premise may have reduced probative value.

A gap in treatment can be relevant, but it is not automatically dispositive. The examiner should consider competent reports of symptoms and explain their medical significance rather than substitute silence in the records for a complete analysis.

Practical takeaway: Before choosing a review option or submitting more evidence, compare the rating decision with the records VA actually considered. The most useful next step addresses the precise missing legal or factual element.

Choose the review lane that matches the deficiency

If the examiner ignored evidence already in the record or VA failed to obtain a necessary opinion, a Higher-Level Review may be appropriate to identify an error based on the prior record. If the claim genuinely needs a new medical nexus opinion, a Supplemental Claim with new and relevant evidence may be more effective. The two approaches solve different problems.

Preserve applicable one-year review deadlines when possible. A late Supplemental Claim can still be available, but the potential effective date may change.

Direct, secondary, and presumptive theories differ

A nexus dispute can arise under direct service connection, secondary service connection, or a presumptive theory. Under 38 C.F.R. § 3.310, a secondary opinion may need to address both causation and aggravation. For a qualifying presumptive condition, the law may supply the connection when the required service and diagnosis criteria are met. Do not demand a traditional nexus opinion for a presumption that removes that requirement.

The correct theory depends on the veteran's service, diagnosis, and evidence. Multiple theories may be reasonably raised, but each should be analyzed on its own terms.

Build the record around the denied element

A focused submission can include a detailed chronology, medical records, competent lay statements, deployment or personnel documentation, and a well-reasoned opinion addressing the prior denial. Ask the clinician to review the specific unfavorable examination and explain any disagreement with its factual or medical assumptions.

More paperwork is not automatically better evidence. The objective is to resolve the precise uncertainty that caused the denial while keeping the procedural history and effective date intact.

Questions to ask before you file

  • What exactly did VA concede in its favorable findings, and what element remains disputed?
  • Which evidence was already in the record when VA made the decision?
  • Is the issue a legal or factual mistake, missing development, or a genuine lack of supporting evidence?
  • What is the date of the decision notice, and which review deadline applies?
  • Would the proposed evidence answer the specific reason VA gave for its decision?
